Deconstructing the Small Group & Individual Instruction Playbook

An instructional coach’s breakdown of MAISA / GELN Essential Practice #3—translating flexible, code-emphasized, meaning-centered literacy instruction into classroom look-fors and collaborative teacher debriefs.

Whole-group Tier I instruction establishes shared grade-level concepts, yet research demonstrates it cannot meet every reader’s and writer’s needs on its own. Small group and individual instruction bridges that gap only when groups are formed around observed and assessed literacy needs—never perceived general ability or static book levels.

Coaching North Star

Essential #3 × Essential #9: Coaching starts with diagnostic and formative assessment data, connects directly to Tier I standards and district-approved high-quality instructional materials (PA 146), and anchors every group in meaning-making.

Informed by the Michigan Dyslexia Handbook (Ch. 4: The Reading Brain, Simple View of Reading, Reading Learning Progression) and LETRS data-driven differentiation.

01 · Equity Scope

Every Child

Receives targeted, explicit instruction during small group or individualized sessions based on observed and assessed literacy needs.

02 · Infrastructure

Every Classroom

Dedicates the time, behavioral routines, and assessment structures required to deliver research-aligned differentiated instruction.

03 · Cadence

Every Day

Provides intentional small group or individual coaching that prioritizes active reading, writing, and fluency over busywork.

Section 01 Core Shift & Legal Guardrails
Research Anchor: Connor et al. (2011)

Flexible Skill Groups Replace Permanent Reading Levels

Connor et al. (2011) established that classrooms delivering differentiated small-group instruction consistently outperform those relying solely on whole-group teaching—especially for learners not yet performing at grade level.

What Essential #3 Is (Teacher-Led) What Essential #3 Isn’t
A component of Tier I instruction for all students. Reserved exclusively for Tier II and Tier III intervention.
Flexible groups that are frequently adjusted as skills shift. Long-term, static, or permanent ability groups.
Grouped by identified assets, skills, standards-based needs, and interests. Grouped by a book level (e.g., “Level J Group”).
Connected to ELA standards or reinforcing/reteaching Tier I whole-group lessons. Isolated instruction disconnected from Tier I or Tier II core work.
Targeted instruction tailored to shared group or individual needs. Repeating the exact same lesson to every small group in rotation.
Direct, explicit instruction with modeling and responsive feedback. Uninstructed practice time or passive check-ins.
Majority of minutes spent reading, writing, and discussing text. Majority of teacher-led time spent on games, worksheets, or teacher talk.
Integrates a meaning-making or comprehension component. Isolated word reading divorced from purpose or meaning.
Code-Emphasis Mandate (MI Dyslexia Law)

Instructional methods must use a code-emphasis approach: direct, explicit instruction on written English at the sound, syllable, morpheme, and word level to build accurate sound-symbol automaticity.

Teachers must NOT use these 5 decoding practices (unless confirming meaning after decoding has been attempted):

  1. Prompting pupils to guess unknown words using pictures and illustrations.
  2. Skipping over an unknown word to use passage meaning to recognize it.
  3. Identifying only the first sound and guessing from initial sound plus context.
  4. Memorizing a word solely in its whole written form.
  5. Using predictable or leveled text for initial word recognition instruction and letter-sound practice.

Section 02 Classroom Ecosystem & Independence
Source: IES Behavioral Interventions Guide & Wright / Tortorelli (2024)

What the Rest of the Class Does Protects the Small Group Table

Teachers can only coach uninterrupted when early-year procedures, clear transition signals, and meaningful independent literacy tasks allow the rest of the room to run smoothly.

Independent Literacy Task

Repeated & Dyad Reading

Students reread texts they can decode independently with changing purposes (character traits, summarizing, highlighting letter-sounds), or read side-by-side in dyads and partner collaborations.

Independent Literacy Task

Writing & Brief Handwriting

Writing in response to reading, making predictions, gathering content-area information, explaining steps, collaborating on peer writing, and brief targeted handwriting practice.

Independent Literacy Task

Applied Skills & Technology

FCRR Student Center Activities (word sorts, word hunts, blending/segmenting, multisyllabic practice, reader’s theater, book clubs), audiobook read-alongs, and research-proven adaptive software.

Three Structures for Moving Independently Through Centers
1. Rotation

All children move through all designated literacy centers on a predictable chime or visual schedule.

2. Choice

Students select which literacy centers to complete, increasing agency and ownership of independent practice.

3. Must Do & May Do

Children complete required core literacy tasks first (“Must Do”), then choose among enrichment options (“May Do”).

Section 03 Instructional Delivery Engine
PA 146 (2024) · Archer & Hughes (2011) · Duke et al. (2011)

Explicit Instruction & the Gradual Release of Responsibility

Under Michigan Public Act 146, explicit instruction is direct, deliberate, and built on continuous teacher-pupil interaction—combining clear explanation, modeling, multiple practice rounds with immediate feedback, distributive practice, and cumulative review.

Interactive Schematic · Select an Instructional Phase

How the 13 Key Elements Map Across Gradual Release

Look For · Teacher
  • Reviews prior content via retrieval practice.
  • Delivers clear explanations of critical content and goals.
  • Sequences instruction into understandable chunks.
  • Maintains an opening, body, conclusion at a brisk pace.
  • Elicits frequent student responses and releases responsibility (I Do, We Do, You Do).
  • Gives specific, informative feedback while monitoring closely.
Look For · Children
  • Articulate what they are learning, why, and how they will know they learned it.
  • Respond continuously through verbal, nonverbal, and written modes.
  • Active participation: no opt-out.
  • Work collaboratively and in partnerships.
  • Engage in deliberate, spaced practice and act on immediate feedback.
Look For · Environment
  • Desks/tables arranged so students face the teacher and can pair up easily.
  • Immediate access to whiteboards, markers, and instructional tools.
  • Behavioral and instructional routines visibly established.
  • Goals and expectations pre-corrected and continuously monitored.

Bullet 1 & Bullet 5 · Minute Allocation Lens

Where Do Small-Group Minutes Actually Go?

Coaches observe how students spend their minutes at the small-group table—guarding against lengthy picture walks, excessive teacher talk, and low-value skill-and-drill worksheets.

The Bullet 5 Equity Imperative

“The children who need the best instruction often get the least.” Research warns that the lowest-performing groups are frequently subjected to skill-and-drill worksheets, isolated word calling, and significantly less actual reading.

Coaches must verify that every group experiences rich text, rigorous thinking, and meaning-making as the ultimate goal—never reserving comprehension solely for higher-performing readers.
